## Post 1 (week 1): the obligation that is already in force

Most QA teams I talk to think AI regulation is something coming.

It is not coming. Part of it is already here.

Since 2 February 2025, Article 4 of the EU AI Act requires every company that uses an AI system to make sure the people using it
have a sufficient level of AI literacy. Not only the companies that build AI, but the ones that use it. If someone in your QC lab
pastes a deviation into a chatbot to "tidy the wording", your company is a deployer.

So the question for a quality system is not "do we use AI?" It is:

→ Do we know where it is used?
→ Who decided that use is acceptable?
→ Where is the training record?
→ Who reviews the output before it becomes a GMP record?

Most sites I know can't answer the first question yet. That's not negligence. Nobody has asked them. But an inspector will, and so
will an auditor, a customer or a QP.

The first step costs nothing: list every place AI touches your documents, data and decisions. Official and unofficial.

How many entries would your list have?

## Post 2 (week 2): what draft Annex 22 actually asks for

In July 2025 the EU and PIC/S published something the GMP world had not had before: a draft annex dedicated to artificial
intelligence, Annex 22, alongside drafts of a revised Annex 11 and Chapter 4.

Reading it as a QC person, I see less a "new AI rulebook" and more the logic of validation we already know, applied to a system
that learns:

→ define the intended use, precisely;
→ test it on data that is independent and representative;
→ set acceptance criteria before you look at the results;
→ keep a human in the loop where the risk calls for it;
→ put the model under change control, and monitor its performance after go-live.

None of that is exotic. What is new is that it must be done for tools many teams adopted informally, in a week, without a
validation plan.

The practical gap I see: teams can describe what their AI tool does, but not how they would know when it is wrong.

That is the question worth answering before the text is final. *(Status: draft at the time of writing; check the current version.)*

How does your site decide whether an AI output can be trusted?

## Post 3 (week 3): the demonstration, and the invitation

How do you know an AI is good enough to check certificates of analysis?

Not by reading its answers and feeling reassured. By testing it against defects you planted yourself.

So I built a fictional company: 200 batches, 800+ laboratory certificates, nearly 4,000 results. Every name, laboratory and
number is invented; every limit is modelled on Ph. Eur. texts. Hidden inside are 36 defects of the kind that reach inspectors:
results out of specification, a certificate page that is missing, two laboratories that disagree, a sub-lot certificate filed
under the wrong code, a result dated after packaging, one certificate number used twice.

An AI reviewer is useful only if it finds all 36 and invents nothing else. That is what Annex 22's logic of test data and
acceptance criteria means in practice.
